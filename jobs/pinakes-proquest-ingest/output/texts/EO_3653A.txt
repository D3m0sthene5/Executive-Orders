ARTICLE XXVIII.
PROTECTION AND PROMOTION OF AMERICAN TRADE.
589. Scope of Duties-It is the duty of consular officers to -protect and
promote the national economic interests of the United States. To this end they
are required-
(I) To endeavor to create, each in. his own district, a situation favorable
to the importation and sale of American goods (general measures of trade
promotion);
(2) To submit frequent reports on commercial and other economic sub-
jects, both voluntarily and in reply to particular instructions from the Secretary
of State;
(3) To reply to the private inquiries of American citizens touching com-
mercial and industrial matters; and
(4) To lend aid and advice to American citizens having or contemplating
business in their several districts.
The emphasis in trade promotion should be upon the development of the
export trade of the United States; but the mutuality of trade interests and the
benefits flowing from a well-balanced commercial exchange, make it also desir-
able that American merchants and manufacturers be assisted in purchasing
abroad raw materials and goods which are not in direct competition with the
products of American industry.
59o. Study of distric.-A consular officer should confine his commercial
activities, and especially his trade reporting, to matters pertaining to his district.
As prerequisite to the proper fulfillment of his commercial functions, he will
familiarize himself, as soon as possible after arrival at his post, with the main
facts constituting the economic life of his district. The manner in which inf.'-
mation is obtained must necessarily vary, but none of the following methods
may properly be omitted:
(I) Study of the more important reports of the officer's predecessor, espe-
cially those covering the last year.
(2) Perusal of other available published reports, monographs and books of
a dependable character containing information concerning the country or
district.
(3) Examination of statistical and other reports, pertaining to the country
or district, published currently by the local government.
(a) Regular reading of the better newspapers and trade journals.
(5) Personal contact with leading local business men and officials.

GENERAL MEASURES OF TRADE PROMOTION.
591. Enforcement of treaty and other rights.-The United States has treaties
with many countries securing within their territories to American citizens
general liberty of commerce and navigation. Consular officers in a country
with which there is such a treaty should study carefully its provisions, and watch
over their observance by the local government. Even where no treaty exists,
they should be vigilant to detect acts detrimental to the interests of American
trade, and of a kind to be considered inconsistent with international commercial
comity. Consular officers are empowered by treaty in the case of some coun-
tries, and in general they are conceded the right by usage, to make complaint
to the local authorities concerning the infraction of treaties, and for the purpose
of protecting the rights and interests of their countrymen. When consular
officers feel they have reason to believe that treaty rights of American citizens
have been infringed, a report should be promptly submitted to the Department
of State and to the supervising consul general, if there be one, or to the American
diplomatic representative. Consular officers will also submit to the supervising
consul general, or to the diplomatic representative, if there be no consul general,
and to the Department of State any general information or observations they
may have to impart touching the rights and interests of American commerce
under treaty and usage.
592. Removal of obstacles to free importation of American goods.-Local laws
and administrative regulations should have the careful attention of consular offi-
cers, more particularly those having to do with the tariff, the customs and port
administrations, warehousing and transportation, quarantine and the sanitary
control of food stuffs. They should also study ocean and inland freight rates,
and other conditions of transport. When it is thought that there is discrimina-
tion against American goods in respect to any of these matters, or that modifica-
tions in the substance, or manner of application of such regulations or rates,
would stimulate the sale of American goods, and the desired modiAfications can
not readily be obtained by application to the local authorities, a full report,
with explicit recommendations as to the modifications considered desirable,
should be made to the supervising consul general, if there be one, or to the
diplomatic representative, if there be no consul general, and to the Department
of State.
593. Creation of demand for American goods.-In addition to facilitating
the importation and sale of American goods in their respective districts, as
indicated in the foregoing paragraphs, consular officers should endeavor to
create and foster an active demand for them on the part of local merchants.
The acquaintance of importers should be carefully cultivated, and the high
order of merit which in the main attaches to all classes of exports from the
United States should be impressed upon them whenever fitting opportunity
offers. The consulate should be made known to the local business community
as a center to which it may resort for information of all kinds respecting
American goods. Statistical and other publications relating to American
trade should be placed at the disposal of all serious inquirers. In particular,
directories of American manufacturers should be displayed for convenient
reference, and every facility afforded in connection with their use.
594. American chambers of commerce abroad.-American chambers of com-
merce, or similar organizations, have been formed in a number of the more
important commercial centers abroad by American citizens and others having
an interest in American business. As these organizations are normally impor-
tant and helpful factors in the development of trade between the United States
and foreign countries, consular officers should lend them hearty support and
cooperation in all their proper activities. In places where such organizations

3
do not already exist and the-number of American residents and the importance
of American business seem sufficient to support them, consular officers should,
within the limitations mentioned below, take an active part, or even assume
the initiative, in any movement for their creation, provided that the projectcdi
organization, by its serious, dependable and truly American character, com-
mends itself to their judgment as being likely to subserve the development of
American trade and the national interests of the United States. In cities where
it is impracticable to organize active chambers of commerce, the formation of
various types of American societies or clubs may be encouraged with a view to
helpful exchanges of opinion and common action in matters of special interest
to the American community.
Officers are cautioned to avoid lending support to unworthy enterprises
and to avoid in any case becoming so closely identified with any chamber of
commerce, or similar organization, that they may be considered by the local
public, or firms or persons in the United States, as sponsors for the business
reliability and financial integrity of the members of the organization.. For this
reason consular officers are not permitted to accept active offices in chambers
of commerce, or similar organizations, but they may, and in appropriate circum-
stances should, act in honorary or advisory capacities. The law forbids the use
of any public funds for the payment of membership dues in any society or
association, unless authorized by specific appropriation.-37 Stat. L. 184.
Consular -fficers while in the United States may not solicit memberships
for chambers of commerce in their districts, or contributions thereto, but may
inform interested persons of the facts relating to the establishment, organiza-
tion, and operation of these organizations, conditions of membership, dues,
etc. They should refer any who may consider application for membership to
the secretary or other appropriate officer of the organization with whom they
may correspond.
COMMERCIAL REPORTS AND REPORTING.
595. Object and general character.-The main object in view in the prepara-
tion of commercial reports should be to supply accurate information for the
use of the Government and the American business community in a general
expansion of American trade.
Such reports should present a faithful reflex of +he actual conditions of
which they treat. Consuls should not yield to any temptation to construe
facts or figures in advocacy of their individual theories and opinions. They
should not, on the other hand, be mere transmitters of objective data. In
addition to setting forth plainly and impartially statistics and positive infor-
mation, the reporting officer should furnish, in distinct paragraphs, an analyt-
ical interpretation of the material presented, and, when appropriate, a full
statement of his personal deductions and opinions.
Data must be obtained, as far as possible, from official sources, and the
origin must in all cases be clearly indicated. When quotations are given, they
should be carefully designated as such with the proper quotation marks, in
order that they may not be confused with individual views or conclusions by
the writer. In those portions of reports which are intended for publication,
reference should be omitted to political or controversial matters, and particular
care should be exercised to avoid unfavorable comments or criticism upon the
government, people, or institutions of the country of the consul's residence.
It is a part of a consul's duty to transmit promptly to the Department of
State commercial information of a political or controversial nature whenever it
appears that the interests of the United States, or of American commerce, may
be subserved thereby; but such communications should be in the form of sepa-rate despatches or special sections of reports conspicuously marked "Confiden-
tial ", intended for the information of the Government alone.

596. Reports to be submitted only to Department of State.-The statutes on
the subject-R. S. Sec. 1712; 32 Stat. L. 827.--contemplate that the Depart-
ment of State shall be the sole medium through which consular reports shall
reach other governmental departments or the public. Consular officers are
accordingly prohibited from furnishing copies of their reports, or articles upon
the trade or commerce of their districts, to any publication, to private persons,
or directly to other departments. Such reports will be transmitted only to the
Department of State, and the fact that they have been prepared should not be
disclosed without special permission until after they have been published.
597. Annual report.-Every consular officer in charge of a consular office
is required to submit to the. Secretary of State, as soon as possible after the close
of each calendar year, a report on the development of commerce and industries
in his district during the year in question. This Annual Report should present
a concise summary of the economic progress or retrogression during the year ini
question of the country or consular district under consideration. It should be
prepared at the earliest possible moment after the annual trade statistics of the
country or district are available. Consular officers should acquaint themselves
with the sources of these statistics and should endeavor to obtain advance.
copies whenever practicable. The statistical tables published by the foreign
governments should be sent as enclosures with the Annual Report, and only the
important statistical tables and summaries need be reproduced in the report
itself. The tables used in the Annual Report should give the figures for the
year under review and the preceding year only, but it is also desirable to set
forth figures for total imports and exports covering a series of years.
The Annual Report should be confined largely to comment upon the out-
standing features of the year's trade and should dwell upon industrial conditions
and development only in brief form and in case they affect American trade in
some important degree.
597a. Periodical Reports.-In order that the Department may be kept fully
informed throughout the year upon all economic developments that may be of
interest to the American business public concise periodical reports upon the
commerce and industries of each consular district are required. They should
contain a general picture of the developments which have taken place within
the district during the period under review. These reports will be used for the
information of the Department of State, and by the Bureau of Foreign and
Domestic Commerce in the preparation of its periodical reviews of various
countries and geographical units.
598. Annual declared export return.-Within twenty days after the close of
each calendar year a return shall be submitted in duplicate to the Department
of State, on Form No. 20, of the exports declared for shipment to the United
States and its dependencies during the year in question at the consular office
and the agencies subordinate to it, if any. This return is in the nature of a
commercial report, and is not to be included in the accounts and returns but
forwarded separately. The return shall include the following separate state-
ments or tables: (i) Itemized quantities and values of exports to the United
States; (2) same for Porto Rico; (3) same for Hawaii; (4) same for the Philip-
pine Islands; and (5) itemized quantities and values of returned American goods,
if any. When there have been no exports to any one or more of the dependen-
cies mentioned, the fact should be specifically stated on the return. Returns
of exports declared at consular agencies should be separate from, but should
be prepared in the same manner as the returns from principal officers.
599. Special reports.-From time to time the Secretary of State addresses
to consular officers circular or particular instructions calling for reports on
special subjects. In complying with these instructions officers should in every
instance endeavor to be prompt in reply, and accurate in statement. Whenever

it seems likely that the gathering of the necessary data will involve delay, theDepartment should be advised at once of the reasons therefor, and the date atwhich the report will probably be forwarded.

REPLIES TO TRADE INQUIRIES.
6o3. Trade inquiries to be answered promptly and specifically.-Inquiries oncommercial subjects received from American citizens, or those acting in theinterest of American trade, should be answered promptly, specifically, and fullywithin the limitations stated in the two succeeding paragraphs. The interestof the inquirer in the development of export trade is usually serious, and adecision whether to enter particular fields may rest upon the replies receivedfrom consular officers. There is need, therefore, for carefully considered, per-
tinent and comprehensive information. In cases where a complete answercan not be given at once, prompt acknowledgment should be made, to be fol-lowed later by a more detailed reply.
6o3a. Limitations on replies to trade inquiries-Consular officers may notcorrespond with private persons as to the political affairs of foreign governments(Paragraph 436.) They must not comment unfavorably upon the institutionsor acts of the Government of the country in which they are assigned, nor givepublicity to opinions or statements of :act which reflect unfavorably on thecustoms and manners of the people.
Requests by private persons or firms for information of any kind relatingto the business of competing Americani firms should be refused. In describingmarket conditions to inquirers care should be exercised to avoid mention byname of other American products or of American firms competing in the same
market.
Consular officers should, in no circumstances, disclose information aboutAmerican firms or their business to other American firms, whether or not theyare competitors in the same line of goods. However, this should i'n no wiseprevent officers from giving full information concerning foreign markets, namesof importers, rates of duties, and specific suggestions for the extension of traderelations. Consular officers should discreetly reply to requests for information
about other American firms to the effect that such information should be pro-cured from the appropriate American manufacturer or head office in the United
States.
The records of American citizens registered at consulates, and records ofinvoices certified, are confidential, and must not be used as sources of informa-tion in replying to inquiries.

6
Consular officers should refuse requests for expression of opinion as to the
value of trade publications of all kinds, and as to the merits of particular articles
which are sold under trademarks or are patented. Replies to these requests
should be of such a character as to preclude their being used for or construed
as testimonials.
6o3b. Use of name as reference; financial standing of foreign firms.--Consular
officers are forbidden to vouch for the financial standing and for the general
responsibility of business men or enterprises. They may, however, refer such
inquirers to banks or other business agencies, and in replying should, whenever
practicable, discreetly give information regarding the general reputation of the
firms or individuals as to whom inquiry has been made, the number of years
they have been established, the general character and volume of their business,
and other facts of the same nature, such as may be readily ascertained.
6o3c. Special lists of foreign firms.-Special lists of foreign firms interested
in specific lines of trade should be prepared for use in connection with replies to
trade inquiries. When necessary a list should be prepared especially to meet
the exact needs of the inquirer. Lists may be arranged, in the discretion of the
consular officer, in the order either of the importance and reputability of the
firms listed, or of their probable usefulness to the inquirer.
6o3d. Waiver of responsibility.-Every list of foreign persons or firms which
is furnished to an inquirer by a consular officer with a view to the establishment
of business relations must be headed by an express waiver of responsibility,
stating that the consular officer can accept no responsibility in any way for the
persons and firms named, and suggesting means by which the inquirer may
inform himself in this respect, such as banks, business organizations, or reporting
agencies.
6o3e. Information sheets to accompany trade letters.-In order to shorten
the replies to trade inquiries and facilitate the investigations of the inquirers,
consular officers are directed to prepare, and transmit with each trade letter an
information sheet, setting forth the main facts of commercial importance
respecting their districts.
6o3f. To be transmitted through Department.-All consular correspondence
touching matters of trade in any way whatsoever must be forwarded to the
addressees through the Department of State, unless otherwise specifically
authorized by the Department.
OTHER DIRECT SERVICES.
603g. Aid and advice to citizens visiting district.-Consular officers will give
all proper aid and advice to American citizens visiting their districts on com-
mercial and other business, and to the traveling salesmen and other representa-
tives abroad of American manufacturers and exporters. They should endeavor
to facilitate in every proper way the legitimate business in connection with
American trade interests which these persons have in hand, not only by furnish-
ing them with needful information, but by aiding them to establish relations
with business firms and officials with whom it is necessary or desirable that they
become acquainted. At the same time they will carefully avoid the appear-
ance of lending official endorsement to the enterprises in which these persons
are interested.
6o3h. Not to act as agent.-Consular officers are forbidden by law to assume
in any way the role of business agent for particular concerns. They must not
solicit orders for them, nor undertake the distribution of samples or printed
matter, although catalogues, and, when the facilities of the office permit, small
samples may properly be received for display in the consulate, and the names
of particular firms may be supplied in response to appropriate inquiries. They
must not execute purchasing commissions of importance for private firms or
individuals except at the direction of the Department of State.

7
6o3i. Prosecution of private claims; collection of debts.-It is no part of the
duty of consular officers to attend to the collection of commercial debts or to
the prosecution of other private claims of American citizens in foreign counries,
although in appropriate cases they should use their good offices to effect amicable
adjustments, and in general they should have such relationships in the busi-
ness communities of their districts as will enable them to mediate successfully
in bringing about an amicable adjustment. When mediation has, however,
proved unsuccessful, or when the consul apprehends that his interference would
be inexpedient, or might be detrimental to the standing and dignity of the
consulate, he should limit his action to furnishing the names of reputable per-
sons competent to act for the inquirer in the premises, such as lawyers, banks,
or collection agencies.
6o3j. Complaints against American exporters.-When complaints are made
against American exporters by their foreign customers, in apparent good faith,
consular officers should endeavor to effect a satisfactory adjustment, with a
view to protecting the national commercial reputation of the United States.
They may, within their discretion, write directly to the American parties in
interest, transmitting the letter as usual through the Department of State.
Such letters should include a full statement of the facts ascertainable, together
with copies of all pertinent correspondence and documents. They should take
the form of a request for information or explanation, and carefully avoid an
assumption of dereliction on the part of the addressees. When it is felt that the
difficulty can not be handled in this manner, the consular officer will transmit
the data concerning the trade dispute under a covering despatch, enclosing a
separate memorandum setting forth in detail the allegations of the complainant
together with any evidence furnished in Siupport thereof. The covering despatch
should furnish the consular officer's views and deductions with regard to the
subject matter of the complaint. The memorandum should contain the facts
of the case in such form as to permit its submission to the American firm con-
cerned. Both the covering despatch and the memorandum should be for-
warded to the Department in triplicate.
603k. Surveys.-Consular officers are sometimes asked to appoint boards of
survey to examine and report on the condition of American merchandise im-
ported into foreign countries of which complaint has been made by the im-
porters. A consul has no statutory authority in this respect, and the findings
of such a board would be without conclusive effect in the United States.
Nevertheless, if the local law does not forbid, consuls should accede to requests
of this character, using their best efforts and judgment to find impartial experts
competent to the work in hand. The findings of the board may be forwarded
by the Consul, through the Department of State, to the exporter in the United
States, together with any comments he may consider appropriate. Oaths of
the surveyors to their findings are unnecessary, but may be administered when
requested by the party calling for the survey, a fee of $2.00 (item 33 of the
tariff of fees) being charged.
603-1. Trade conferences.-When consular officers visit thc United States
personal conferences may be arranged, in appropriate circumstances, between
them and American manufacturers and exporters, in order that the latter may
learn by direct interchange, of the opportunities for the establishment or exten-
sion of their trade in the officer's district. Consular officers may at the same
time learn from American manufacturers and exporters the viewpoint o~ the
American seller, his facilities for handling foreign trade, and his requirements
and expectations. Consular officers are required, before leaving their posts for
the United States, to prepare themselves especially for these interviews; and,
upon their return from the United States, or immediately before their departure
therefrom, to submit to the Department of State a detailed report of con-
ferences held.

COOPERATION IN TRADE PROMOTION.
6o3m. As the success of trade promotion work depends to a great extent
upon coordination of effort, the fullest cooperation is required between consuls,
supervising consuls general, and the diplomatic missions; and the cooperative
spirit shown by individual consular officers in adjusting their efforts to the
demands of national or regional coordination will be considered in determining
their efficiency ratings in trade promotion.
As an important part of their functions supervising consuls general are
charged with the general direction of trade promotion activities in their respec-
tive jurisdictions, and are authorized, subject to the approval of the Department
of State, to issue to the consuls resident therein instructions designed to obviate
duplication of effort, and to give harmony to their commercial activities.
Supervising consuls general should not, however, exercise their authority
in such a manner as unduly to discourage initiative or voluntary effort on the
part of the consuls in their jurisdictions. The special knowledge, judgment,
and interest possessed by each principal consular officer with~ regard to his
district must be recognized, and, so far as may be consistent with the avoidance
of duplication and unnecessary effort, each such officer must be given unlimited
opportunity for the preparation of reports on all matters arising in his district
and primary responsibility for the protection and promotion of American trade
therein.
Commercial attaches, as members of the diplomatic missions, like other
members thereof, have no formal relations with consular officers except
through the chiefs of mission. Such correspondence as a chief of mission may
authorize a commercial attache to conduct in his own name with consular
officers must pass through the supervising consul general.
