# How executive orders approving NIRA codes of fair competition ended (shared analysis, 2026-10-29)

Producer: Claude (Opus 5.5). Independent review: a separate-context Claude (Opus 5.5) reviewer checked ten
propositions against the held sources, upheld four, upheld five with corrections that are incorporated here,
and rejected the one planted false proposition (`review-shared/BLIND_REVIEW.json`, adjudication in
`review-shared/ADJUDICATION.json`). Scope: executive orders whose operative command approves, prescribes,
amends, modifies, stays or grants an exemption under a code of fair competition under section 3 of Title I of
the National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA). Each order still needs its own reading for its own terms, earlier
end, independent commands and surviving effects. This note sets no status, date or claim for any order and
certifies nothing (certified count: 0).

## Conclusion

For a code-dependent command that did not end earlier, the best supported first end is **June 14, 1935**, by
statutory repeal. That day Congress approved Senate Joint Resolution 113 (Pub. Res. No. 26, 74th Cong., 49
Stat. 375, chapter 246). Its section 2 repealed "all the provisions of Title I of such Act delegating power to
the President to approve or prescribe codes of fair competition and providing for the enforcement of such
codes". The resolution states no effective date other than its approval.

The repeal extinguished the prospective operative effect of every such command. Under NIRA as enacted, an
approved code had legal effect only through section 3: under 3(b) its provisions became the standards of fair
competition for the trade or industry, and a violation in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce was an
unfair method of competition under the Federal Trade Commission Act; under 3(c) the district courts could
restrain violations and district attorneys were directed to sue; under 3(f) each violation was a misdemeanor.
Section 3(d) let the President prescribe a code with the same effect where none had been approved, and section
7(c) let him prescribe a limited code of hours, wages and working conditions. Section 2 of the resolution
repealed, by description rather than by section number, the power to approve or prescribe codes and every
provision through which an approved code was enforced. It therefore supplies the reason that the owner's
2026-10-28 ruling requires before a later general rule may end a command; its date is not used as a default.

June 16, 1935 is not an end date. It was Title I's original two-year expiry under section 2(c), but section 1
of the same resolution replaced that expiry with April 1, 1936, and nothing in the resolution defers section 2.
EO 7075 of June 15, 1935 already acts under Title I "as amended by" the resolution.

## Validity under Schechter

A. L. A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States (295 U.S. 495), decided May 27, 1935, held "the code-making
authority thus conferred" by section 3 "an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power" and, on that
ground and on the separate ground that the provisions regulated intrastate transactions affecting interstate
commerce only indirectly, held the Live Poultry Code provisions in question invalid (No. 854 reversed; No. 864
affirmed). The delegation holding reaches every section 3 approval or prescription.

Every claim records Schechter as a **validity characterization**, not as an end date. Under the doctrine
prevailing in 1935, an approval made under an unconstitutional delegation was void from issuance, and May 27,
1935 is the date that invalidity was judicially declared. The judgment did not cancel or withdraw any other
approving order, and later doctrine treats an invalid measure's prior existence as an operative fact, so the
recorded end of the command's operative effect remains June 14, 1935. May 27, 1935 becomes an end date for a
specific code only if a held record shows that the government cancelled or suspended that code on that date.
The June 14 conclusion does not change under any of the readings the review considered; only a code-specific
cancellation record would move the end earlier.

## Surviving effects

No held source continues any code approval after June 14, 1935.

- **Accrued consequences.** Fines, injunctions, convictions and acts done under a code before the repeal are
  completed effects. The general savings statute of the time (not a held source) preserved liabilities
  already incurred under a repealed statute; that preservation is an accrued effect and does not keep the
  command operative. No held source shows a prosecution continued after June 14, 1935.
- **Antitrust exemption.** Section 5 exempted from the antitrust laws any code, agreement or license approved
  and in effect under Title I, and any complying action taken during that period, while the title was in
  effect and for sixty days after. The resolution's proviso states that the exemption "shall extend only to
  agreements and action thereunder" that put section 7(a) requirements into effect or prohibit practices
  already unlawful. The proviso names agreements, not codes, and states no time limit; the held text does not
  settle whether it narrowed the exemption for action taken under codes before June 14, 1935. Whatever
  exemption survives for past action is an accrued effect, not continued operation of the approval.
- **Later statutes.** Statutes that later regulated an industry (for example the Bituminous Coal Conservation
  Act of 1935) are independent laws and do not continue the order.

## Denials of exemption and other one-time determinations

An order that denies an application for an exemption from a code, or dismisses a complaint, commands
nothing after it issues. Its command is exhausted by performance on the date it is signed (or on a later
stated effective date). The applicant's continuing obligation after the denial is the code's own operation,
which the approving order governs and which ended with the repeal; it is not a surviving command of the
denial. Record such a clause as exhausted on its date, with the June 14, 1935 repeal as a later event. A stay
or exception inside the same order (for example "except as stayed by the Administrator") is a separate
clause with its own end. This rule was added after the first independent review, so each batch reviewer
tests it on the orders it reaches.

## Events that do not end a code approval

- **April 1, 1936.** The expiry of Title I as amended ended what remained of Title I (for example section 4(a)
  agreement authority and NRA administration). For a code approval it is a later event.
- **EO 7075 (June 15, 1935)** terminated the National Industrial Recovery Board and created the office of
  Administrator of the National Recovery Administration; **EO 7252 (December 21, 1935, effective January 1,
  1936)** terminated the National Recovery Administration. Both concern administration.
- **A later order amending a code** is a later event of the approving order unless its text replaces the code
  or revokes or cancels the approval.

## What each order still needs

1. Read the order's own held text. Confirm that it approves, prescribes, amends, modifies, stays or exempts
   under a named section 3 code, and record every other operative command.
2. Own terms. A stated period, condition, "until" date or provisional approval that ends before June 14, 1935
   is the order's first end; the repeal is then a later event.
3. Earlier government action. Search held order texts for later orders naming the same code or this order. A
   cancellation or modification under section 10(b), a superseding code or a revocation before June 14, 1935
   is the first end of what it reaches.
4. Independent commands. Directions that do not depend on the code need their own end or a block.
5. Categories this note does not decide (the reviewer's list): section 4(a) agreements, including the
   President's Reemployment Agreement; section 7(b) labor-agreement approvals and section 7(c) limited codes;
   section 4(b) licensing orders (section 4(b) expired June 16, 1934); section 3(e) import-limitation orders;
   section 9(c) petroleum orders (Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, January 7, 1935) and mixed
   petroleum-code orders; section 8(b) delegations to the Secretary of Agriculture and instruments actually
   issued under the Agricultural Adjustment Act; organizational and administrative orders under sections 2, 6
   and 10, including EO 7075 and EO 7252; orders issued on or after June 14, 1935; mixed orders whose code
   approval sits beside appointments, delegations or other commands (each command separately); Title II public
   works orders; labor-board orders resting on section 7(a) or other statutes; and the Live Poultry Code's
   approving order, whose adjudicated provisions need separate treatment.
