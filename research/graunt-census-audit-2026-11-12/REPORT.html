Research audit · November 12, 2026 · cutoff October 14, 2026

How many U.S. executive orders?

My best enumerable result is 16,811 distinct-directive proxies: 14,995 numbered and 1,816 unnumbered. These are counted, source-identified records under the decisions below. They are a revised working inventory, not a demonstrated count of every order ever issued or every paper personally signed by a president.

The identified-inventory admission range is 16,400–16,811, calculated under two evidence rules. The higher count, my provisional catalog-based choice, admits 411 power-site date groups whose presidential connection is inferred from an official registry and its statutory history. Omitting those groups gives 14,995 numbered and 1,405 unnumbered proxies. This is not a statistical interval or an upper bound on the historical population.

I cannot defend a finite national range or a single best estimate for the lost and never-catalogued tail. The evidence supports an expanding identified inventory and substantial unresolved missingness. It does not identify the unseen population. Treating that tail as zero, taking a midpoint of old guesses, or accepting the repository’s overlap-model interval would create precision the sources do not support. The workbook therefore separates counted records from explicitly conditional estimates.

Audit workbook · Reconciled identity inventory (CSV)

What I counted

The working historical definition is a distinct written presidential directive identified as an executive order in the numbered series, a contemporary official publication, or a historical executive-order compilation. An express presidential approval or a specific presidential instruction transmitted by a secretary can qualify. A printed text or explicit documentary reference can identify an order without proving that the original autograph survives. A general delegation does not turn every later agency action into another presidential order.

I count a directive once across aliases, republications and multiple source appearances. I group several numbered clauses when the compiled text shows one terminal presidential approval. Different subject entries for the same reservation document are not automatically different orders. Separately dated reissuances remain separate provisionally, even if their wording repeats. Ordinary proclamations, modern memoranda and national-security directives remain separate document families unless there is a specific executive-order-series or historical-catalog basis. Military and ceremonial administrative commands are included but flagged for narrower definitions.

This definition answers “issued or specifically approved,” not “personally signed in the president’s own hand.” The supplied census itself marks authentication and signer verification as unadjudicated. I have not authenticated every inherited record. A literal autograph count would require a different archival project.

The numbered reconciliation

15,009 catalog labels, counted; 14,995 grouped presidential-directive proxies, counted under provisional identity decisions. Starting with 14,979 numbered census rows, I identify 29 absent serial entries and provisionally reclassify the already-counted March 10, 1863 proclamation under its later catalog alias EO 1-A. That reclassification adds no document. It gives 15,009 numbered labels. Grouping Cleveland’s 17 missing serial entries into six documents subtracts 11. Grouping EO 27/27-1 as one instruction subtracts one. Excluding Seward’s two statutory certifications, EOs 6 and 7, subtracts two.

The 24 missing whole numbers are 36–49, 51–53, 92, 2103, 2539, 2703, 3493, 3733 and 6585. EO 50 was already present. All 24 have documentary support; they should not be described as unused numbers. The five additional suffix entries are 130A, 2687A, 5151A, 7010F and 8606A. EO 130A currently has index evidence rather than a read full text. Each finding has its date, subject, evidence level and citation in the workbook.

The Cleveland grouping is supported by the civil-service texts and their shared terminal approvals in Richardson, not simply by proximity of serial numbers. It is still a document reconstruction, not inspection of the signed sheets. EO 27 and 27-1 transmit the same Washburne mourning instruction to different recipient groups; restoring a separate dispatch as a counting unit adds one. Counting each Cleveland serial clause separately adds 11. Conversely, rejecting index-only 130A subtracts one. Excluding the Polk-signed 3016C/K subtracts two; the court describes delegated authority and later Wilson ratification. These selected known choices, holding all other entries fixed, produce a calculated 14,992–15,007 sensitivity range. This is neither a confidence interval nor a complete personal-signature range. A personal-signature rule would also affect other secretary-transmitted orders. The interval does not cover undiscovered suffixes or additional inherited misclassifications and duplicates.

EOs 6 and 7 are signed by Secretary Seward as certifications of the Fourteenth Amendment under a publication statute. The instrument bodies do not establish a presidential order. They remain legitimate archival series entries, which is why the catalog-label count retains them. By contrast, the printed Federal Register expressly records FDR’s approval of Ickes-originated EOs 7757–7759; a secretary’s signature alone is not grounds to reject every such record. Unnumbered additions and source disagreements

The workbook currently contains 3,820 individually sourced finding rows, with 1,817 counted additions: 18 numbered document groups and 1,799 unnumbered proxies. Zero-count rows preserve corroborating sources, numbered aliases, departmental exclusions, definition alternatives and unresolved identities. The separate Source review sheet preserves another 21 TR catalog cross-references and nine historical source errors or variants. These row counts are counted audit outputs, not national population estimates.

The original 19 unnumbered rows reduce to 17 retained identities: reclassify the March 10, 1863 instrument as the EO 1-A alias without adding a document, and merge the two August 8, 1914 entries. Both NARA index appearances point to the same Public Water Reserve No. 22 order amended by EO 8507. EO 8507, 5 FR 2817.

Lord’s chronological pages 1–226 were reviewed across this audit, with the Roosevelt segment reconciled through the TR Presidential Library’s individual records. Richardson volumes VI, VII and IX supplied compiled texts and additional directives. Lord’s reference-only entries are useful evidence of identification, but a citation to a later reservation book does not authenticate a signature or prove that several site entries are separate documents. Lord’s 1943 volume at the Library of Congress; TR Library methods.

Three examples explain why a simple union of lists fails. First, the TR Library has 105 retained unnumbered entries and 21 cross-references: the underlying records resolve those 21 into 19 numbered-EO appearances and two proclamations, despite shorthand prose describing all 21 as numbered duplicates. Second, the census’s October 26, 1874 lighthouse date conflicts with the operative text of Proclamation 2319, which repeatedly identifies October 20. Third, an 1888 Fort Missoula enlargement in a later military-reservation compilation traces to a headnote typo: the Attorney General opinion’s body gives August 5, 1878, already in Lord. Agreement among derivative books can reproduce one error. Proclamation 2319, 53 Stat. 2520.

Other errors are categorical. Lord includes a December 12, 1917 Water Power Designation that a later public-land order identifies as departmental, and a 1934 retirement-transfer action that the Supreme Court attributes to the Civil Service Commission. Pre-July 1910 temporary power withdrawals also require an authorship distinction: a later EO may call an earlier act an “executive order” even where the Supreme Court identifies the Secretary of the Interior as its issuer. Such cases are not automatically presidential additions. The workbook preserves the competing citations and count decisions.

The largest further source is a 1976 USGS waterpower registry. We transcribed 771 reserve claims and 814 distinct revision/date claims, counted as source records, and supplemented them with eight order-specific references. The revision claims consolidate 1,176 image-checked source entries; 69 already match numbered census orders, while 372 are eligible for grouping as possible presidential restorations. Unconfirmed modifications and audit/accounting entries do not generate extra orders. After exclusions and reconciliation with Lord, the working inventory uses 475 catalog-date groups, counted. Of these, 64 have Lord or another order-specific reference; 411 rely on the registry’s PSR/restoration classification plus the authority history in USGS Circular 400. These groups are provisional reconstructions, not authenticated signed instruments. USGS 1976 register; USGS Circular 400, pp. 2–4.

A reserve number is not an executive-order number. The July 2, 1910 Taft confirmation illustrates why: a contemporary USGS publication prints a presidential confirmation covering outstanding withdrawals. I therefore group same-date PSR claims conservatively, preserving individual reserve identities as source claims. This can undercount separately signed orders on other shared dates. The 558 eligible reserve identifiers in the final registry review are not an upper bound on EO documents; additional temporary-withdrawal entries may belong to the same later confirmation, and creation, modification and restoration are different acts. The inventory range above addresses evidence admission, not every unresolved document split. USGS Water-Supply Paper 344, pp. 169–171.

The final restoration review also exposed date and authorship problems. Later primary sources identify seven more reserve entries as departmental, removing six previously inferred date groups. The remaining eligible restoration claims add 175 new catalog dates. A 1919 House document distinguishes agency recommendation dates from presidential approval dates: for example, Restoration 261 was recommended June 19, 1918 and approved July 15. Yet other dates in that approval table conflict with later signed orders. The workbook preserves these competing claims. Consequently, the date-group count can move either upward or downward when originals are inspected; it is not a verified minimum of presidential signatures. H. Doc. 1703, 65th Congress, 3rd session, p. 6.

Unpublished and classified material

394 unique unpublished/not-received numbered identities were counted in 27 retrieved NARA annual tables for 1937–1968; all 394 are already in the census. This is a partial-table audit, not a complete unpublished-order census. The 1957 House study’s 31 confidential orders since 1915 cannot be added wholesale: the specifically named postwar 10026A, 10369A and 10571A are already represented. The study does supply the missing July 1, 1952 date for 10369A. 1957 House study, pp. 40–41.

The July 11, 1941 Coordinator of Information order is a strong new unnumbered example: the Federal Register prints it and GAO calls it an executive order. The military orders establishing OSS and calling Philippine forces into U.S. service are separately identifiable, definition-sensitive directives. The October 24, 1952 NSA memorandum and January 22, 1946 CIG directive are shown as broader functional alternatives, not silently promoted into formal EOs. COI order, 6 FR 3422–3423; CIG directive; NSA memorandum.

A broader functional-directive definition changes the population substantially. GAO reported at least 1,042 NSC presidential directives from 1961 through August 1988, of which 247 had been publicly released. These are reported source counts for another document family, not 1,042 individually audited EO additions. Draft Presidential Emergency Action Documents should not be counted as issued orders without issuance evidence. GAO NSIAD-89-31, pp. 2–4.

What can be estimated about orders nobody can list?

The unseen tail is not identified by the available data. Lord’s preface explicitly says the total of unnumbered orders was unknown and reports Ickes’s 15,000 and others’ 50,000. It supplies no inventory, sampling frame or derivation. Repetition by the House study, CRS and later scholarship is not independent corroboration of those quantities. Adding either guess to today’s numbered series creates a historical scenario, not an estimate validated by this audit. Lord, preface iv; 1974 Senate print, p. 27.

The repository’s two-list Chapman model yields an estimated 4,542.5 unnumbered identities, with a nominal 4,101–5,095 interval. Its own matching-threshold alternatives yield about 3,714 and 9,681. Those are model outputs, not counts. The inputs contain OCR merges, date failures, subject-index text, numbered cross-references and different document units. More fundamentally, Lord compiled the very Richardson, military-reservation and land-office sources being used as a supposed second capture list. Survival and discovery probabilities vary by era, agency and subject. Correcting one matching threshold does not establish independence or equal catchability. The nominal interval therefore does not quantify uncertainty about all actual EOs. Repository model inputs and assumptions.

For transparent sensitivity analysis, let N be the identified numbered count, U the identified unnumbered count and p an assumed share of historical unnumbered orders represented by U. Then estimated unseen = U(1/p − 1), and estimated total = N + U/p. The workbook evaluates these formulas. No evidence currently selects p; the middle scenario is not my preferred estimate. These scenarios assume each retained proxy corresponds to exactly one historical instrument, with no additional false positives or groups concealing multiple documents.

What would most improve the count

The highest-value next step is a document-level inventory of the General Land Office executive-order and proclamation series in RG 49, followed by Interior, War, Navy and Treasury approval records. Resolve the known multi-site and multi-section cases against signed originals or certified copies. Link each document to all its catalog numbers, dates, printed appearances and reservations. The useful unit is the instrument, not the page or the site name. NARA RG 49 guide, especially 49.3.5.

For an actual missing-population estimate, first construct an archival sampling frame, then sample folders or volumes within era and agency strata. Measure presidential-order incidence, distinct-document rates and coverage by the existing inventory. This would provide an observable denominator and estimable uncertainty for the surviving archive within that frame. Sampling surviving folders cannot by itself estimate destroyed or never-recorded orders; lost series need separate evidence and assumptions. Classified modern orders require a separate series/accounting inquiry rather than an arbitrary percentage allowance.
